Governance as a Competitive Edge

Misfer Almaymouni  ·  May 2026

Most organizations treat governance as a compliance cost — a set of policies and committees required by the parent entity, the regulator, or the auditor. You staff a GRC function, produce the reports, pass the review, and move on. The work is real, but the value is defensive at best.

At AQALAT by STC, we tried something different. Over five years managing a real estate portfolio of 1,300+ assets, we built governance not as a control layer on top of the business, but as structural tissue running through it. The result was two consecutive years of leading cybersecurity compliance among all stc subsidiaries and the highest Local Content Index in the group — not because we optimized for those metrics, but because they were natural outputs of an organization that knew how to govern itself.

The separation fallacy

The first thing we challenged was the assumption that Finance, GRC, HR, and Strategy should operate as independent silos reporting separately upward. Each function had its own language, its own rhythm, and its own relationship with the parent entity. The result was a company that could not speak to itself coherently.

We restructured around a unified executive mandate. Not by merging functions — each retained its professional integrity — but by making them accountable to a shared strategic narrative. When Finance, GRC, and Strategy operate from the same understanding of what the organization is trying to become, governance stops being a tax and starts being a coordination mechanism.

Clarity as a governance product

One of the most underrated governance outputs is clarity of accountability. Not org charts — those are static and immediately out of date — but living, negotiated boundaries between what the subsidiary owns, what the parent decides, and what requires joint resolution.

We spent significant time negotiating operating model boundaries with stc. These conversations were uncomfortable. They surfaced assumptions that had been left implicit for years. But the outcome — documented, agreed, and communicated — meant that teams could move without constantly escalating for permission. That speed is a governance dividend.

What this requires from leadership

None of this works if the executive team treats governance as something done to the organization by the compliance function. It has to be owned at the top, modeled in decision-making, and reflected in how leadership talks about the business publicly and internally.

The organizations I have watched struggle with governance almost always have a leadership team that is genuinely surprised when governance failures surface. The signal was always there. Someone in GRC or Finance was raising it through the proper channels. But the channels were not connected to where decisions actually got made.

Governance as a competitive edge is not a framework or a policy set. It is a decision about what kind of organization you want to be — and then the sustained discipline to build the infrastructure that makes that decision real.
