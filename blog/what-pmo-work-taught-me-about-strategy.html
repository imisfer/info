What PMO Work Taught Me About Enterprise Strategy

Misfer Almaymouni  ·  April 2026

I have built project management offices in a 1,200-bed hospital, a global dairy operation, and a real estate portfolio spanning 1,300+ assets. Each environment was radically different. The politics, the timelines, the definition of a successful outcome — none of it transferred directly. But the deeper lesson was the same every time: the quality of your execution infrastructure determines the ceiling on your strategy.

That sounds obvious when stated plainly. In practice, most organizations treat execution infrastructure — PMOs, performance management systems, reporting cadences — as administrative overhead that strategy sits above. The strategy team sets the direction; the PMO tracks whether projects are on time. Clean separation. Tidy org chart. Entirely wrong.

King Fahad Medical City: building before the doors opened

I joined KFMC a year before the complex opened. That timing was formative. We were not improving an existing system — we were deciding, from first principles, what infrastructure a 1,200-bed hospital needed to execute at launch and sustain itself afterward.

The PMO we built was not a project-tracking function. It was the mechanism through which 19 clinical and administrative workstreams stayed coherent with each other and with the inauguration timeline. When a vendor slipped on an HIS module, the PMO was how we understood the downstream impact on three other modules before it became a crisis.

What I learned there: execution infrastructure is most valuable before the organization has formed its habits. The window to build it right is narrow.

Al Safi Dairy: ISO as a forcing function

The ISO-9001 and ISO-14000 certifications we achieved at Al Safi were not the point. They were useful external validation, but the actual value was what the certification process forced the organization to do: map every significant process, assign clear ownership, and produce documented evidence that the work was being done as described.

Process mapping at that scale — the largest integrated dairy farm in the world at the time — is not a documentation exercise. It is a diagnosis. You discover which processes exist only in the knowledge of specific individuals, which ones are described one way in policy and executed another way in practice, and which dependencies have never been made explicit.

The gap between how an organization thinks it operates and how it actually operates is where most strategic initiatives go to die.

The PMO work at Al Safi narrowed that gap. The strategy became more executable not because we got better at strategy, but because we got better at understanding our own organization.

What this means for how I think about strategy now

When I moved into VP-level corporate affairs and strategy roles, I carried a strong prior: the most important question about any strategic initiative is not whether it is directionally correct, but whether the organization has the execution infrastructure to deliver it.

A strategy that outpaces your PMO maturity, your performance management systems, and your governance clarity will not fail loudly. It will diffuse quietly — absorbed into the organization's existing habits, stripped of its ambition, and eventually reported as accomplished when it is really just abandoned in place.

Building execution infrastructure is not the unsexy work you do before the real work begins. It is the real work. Strategy without it is a document.
